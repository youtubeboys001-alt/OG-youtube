Right now, you are alive because of chemistry.

Not metaphorically. Literally. If every drug and every medicine on Earth vanished overnight, a massive share of the people alive today would not survive the year. And there's a real chance you'd be one of them.

You don't feel it. You feel healthy, self-sufficient, like a body that mostly runs on its own. But that feeling is the illusion. Somewhere in the last hundred years, the human species quietly stopped being able to survive on its own — and almost nobody noticed it happen.

So let's ask the uncomfortable question directly. If you took it all away — every pill, every vaccine, every antibiotic — how long would we actually last?

Start with what we tell ourselves. We like to believe humans got healthier over time. That we're a naturally tough species that clawed its way up to long, healthy lives through sheer resilience. That's not what happened.

We didn't get tougher. We built a chemical scaffold underneath the entire species — and then we forgot it was holding us up. Pull the scaffold away, and you finally see what a modern human actually is underneath. And it starts with the single most important drug ever made.

Antibiotics.

Before them, a scratch could kill you. Not a dramatic wound — a scratch. A thorn in the garden. A blister that got dirty. A scraped knee. Any small break in the skin was a door, and on the other side of that door were bacteria that could get into your blood and end you in days. In the pre-antibiotic world, minor infections were a routine cause of death. People died from infected teeth. From cuts. From a sore throat that turned into something worse.

And surgery? Surgery was almost a form of suicide. It didn't matter how well the operation went. You'd survive the knife and then die a week later from the infection that followed.

Here's how thin that line really is. One of the very first people ever treated with penicillin was a police officer in Oxford, in 1941. His crime against nature? He had scratched his face — on a rose thorn. That scratch became infected, the infection spread through his body, and it was killing him. The new drug worked. Within days he was recovering, pulled back from death by a thorn scratch. And then the penicillin ran out. There was so little of it in the world that they couldn't finish his course. The infection returned, and he died. That is the entire pre-antibiotic world in one story — a healthy man, a garden thorn, and a slow, certain death.

Take antibiotics away, and every one of those deaths comes straight back. A world where a rose thorn can kill you. Where a routine appendix operation is a coin flip with your life.

And that's just the injuries. Because it gets far worse at the two most dangerous moments in any human life.

Being born. And giving birth.

For almost all of human history, these were the deadliest things a person ever did. Roughly half of all children died before the age of five. Half. Every family expected to bury children — it was simply part of being alive. And for mothers, childbirth was a genuine gamble with death, every single time. Infection, bleeding, complications with no way to intervene.

Modern medicine didn't gently improve those numbers. It obliterated them. The single biggest reason human lifespan appears to have doubled isn't that adults live much longer — it's that we stopped so many babies and mothers from dying. That entire victory rests on medicine. Pull it, and the graveyards that history was full of begin to refill almost immediately.

And there's another door we've forgotten is even there.

The plagues. Measles. Polio. Smallpox. Diseases so terrifying they shaped human history for thousands of years — and diseases most people now never think about at all. They're not gone because we became stronger. They're held back by a thin, invisible wall of vaccines. Stop maintaining that wall, and they come back — to a population that has no built-up immunity to any of them. In a strange way, that makes us more vulnerable than our ancestors were. They at least lived in a world where survivors carried resistance. We've never met these diseases at all.

But here is the part that makes this genuinely dark. Because so far this is just about losing treatments. This next part is about what medicine did to us.

For all of evolution, there was one law running underneath every living thing. Natural selection. And it was brutal. It killed the weak, the sick, and the vulnerable before they could ever have children — and only the hardiest passed their genes forward. That is the engine that built every species on this planet, including ours.

Medicine switched that engine off. Mercifully. Wonderfully. We decided, as a species, that we would no longer let the vulnerable die — and so we saved them, and they grew up, and they had children of their own. That is one of the most beautiful things humans have ever done.

But it has a consequence almost nobody says out loud. It means the modern human gene pool now carries conditions that used to be automatically lethal. Millions of people are alive right now only because of a drug they take every single day. Insulin for diabetes. Medication for hearts that would otherwise stop. Dialysis for kidneys that no longer work. In the natural world — the one without medicine — none of them would still be here.

That is not a criticism. It's the opposite. It's the measure of how far we've climbed. But it means the moment you remove medicine, natural selection does not return gently. It comes back roaring — fast, indifferent, and merciless. The oldest law on Earth switches back on, and it starts collecting everyone the scaffold was holding up.

So — how long would we last?

Not extinction. Humans are too many and too spread out to vanish. But it would be a crash. A brutal, rapid winnowing in the first years, as infections, childbirth, and untreated disease swept through a population that had completely forgotten how to die from them. And the humans who came out the other side would be fewer, harder, and — over generations — genetically different from us. A tougher species, rebuilt by the same merciless law that built the first one.

And that's the thought that stays with you. You picture yourself as a self-contained body, a survivor, a product of millions of years of hardening. But that's not quite what you are anymore. The thing keeping you alive was never just your body. It's an invisible, fragile chain of chemistry — factories, supply lines, a century of accumulated knowledge — quietly holding up the entire species.

Most people will live their whole lives without ever feeling the weight of it.

Until the day it isn't there.
